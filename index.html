Target Wake Time (TWT) lets an access point (AP) and a station (STA) agree on when the STA wakes up to exchange traffic, so that it can sleep for the rest of the time. In stock Wi-Fi HaLow software, the STA proposes its own TWT schedule when it associates and keeps it for the life of the link. In this testbed, the AP alone sets the TWT schedule of every associated STA and changes it at run time, over the air, without re-association. A separate sniffer then verifies from the air that every STA transmits inside its assigned service period (SP).

1Background: Target Wake Time

A low-duty-cycle IoT device spends most of its energy in idle listening, i.e., keeping its receiver 'ON' while no traffic is addressed to it. Target Wake Time (TWT) lets the AP and a STA agree on a schedule of service periods (SPs) instead. The STA wakes up at the start of its SP, exchanges its traffic with the AP, and remains in sleep state until the next SP.

TWT was first introduced in IEEE 802.11ah (Wi-Fi HaLow), the sub-1 GHz Wi-Fi standard for long-range, low-power IoT, and was later adopted by IEEE 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6). A TWT agreement is defined by three parameters, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Fig. 1. Radio state of one STA of this testbed over 3 s, drawn to scale. A minimum wake duration of 65.28 ms in a 1 s wake interval corresponds to a duty cycle of 6.5%.

Target wake time
The start of the SP, in microseconds on the AP's TSF timer. With a wake interval of 1 s, it acts as an offset within each second.
Wake interval
The time between the starts of two consecutive SPs. After the first SP, the STA computes every next wake time itself by adding the interval.
Minimum wake duration
The minimum time the STA stays awake from the start of each SP, i.e., the nominal length of the SP.

In the stock software of this platform, TWT is negotiated only once, at association. The STA is the TWT requester: it proposes the parameters in its Association Request, the AP responds in the Association Response, and the agreement then holds for the life of the link. Other TWT mechanisms that could change a schedule from the AP side, such as broadcast TWT or TWT Information frames, are not implemented in this stack. Hence, the AP has no means to push a new TWT schedule to an associated STA.

This is the gap that the testbed addresses. The AP sends a new TWT schedule to an associated STA at any time, and the STA adopts it without re-association. Since the AP is the only node that observes all the STAs, it is the natural place for the scheduling decision, e.g., assigning non-overlapping SPs, grouping STAs into a shared SP, or shortening the SPs.

TWT mode used in this testbed
Individualone agreement per STA, not a broadcast schedule
Implicitthe STA computes each next wake time by adding the wake interval

2Objective: unilateral TWT over the air

The objective of this testbed is unilateral TWT over the air: the AP alone decides the TWT schedule of each STA and can change it at any time by sending a TWT Setup frame to that STA. The STA neither requests nor negotiates the new schedule, and it remains associated throughout.

To achieve this, we modified the host software on both sides: one layer on the AP and two layers on the STA, as described in Section 3. The firmware of the MM6108 radio chip, which executes the sleep/wake timing, is left untouched. An updated schedule is installed in it through the same interface as a schedule negotiated at association.

3Testbed

The testbed consists of one AP, one sniffer, six STAs and a host computer. All eight radios use the same chip, the Morse Micro MM6108. The difference is in the host processor: the AP and the sniffer each contain a Raspberry Pi 4 running OpenWrt Linux, whereas each STA is driven by an STM32U585 microcontroller running FreeRTOS. Each STA continuously sends UDP uplink traffic to the AP, about 1 Mbit/s, so that every SP carries traffic and the schedule can be observed on air. Table 1 lists the testbed parameters.

AP driver (morse.ko)

Stock: only responds to a TWT request made by a STA.

  • Control interface. One line written into the debugfs file twt_ap_update, in the form <MAC> <wake_time_us> [interval_us] [duration_us], updates the schedule of one STA while the BSS is running.
  • Unsolicited TWT Setup frame. The driver encodes the new parameters into a TWT element with the setup command Accept and sends it to that STA in a TWT Setup action frame. The wake-interval exponent is recomputed for every update, so that any interval is encoded correctly.
  • Commit on acknowledgement. The driver applies the new parameters to a copy of the agreement and replaces its own record only when the chip reports the STA's ACK. in twt.c, debug.c and mac.c of the driver

B

mmagic agent (STA)

Stock: the STA does not request TWT at all.

  • TWT request with Suggest. At association, the STA now requests TWT with the setup command Suggest, i.e., it proposes a schedule and takes the one returned by the AP.
  • Uplink traffic at boot. The STA starts its iperf UDP client at every boot and keeps it running, so that a board on a power bank, with no serial connection, always has data to send.

in mmagic_core_wlan.c and mmagic_core_iperf.c

C

morselib, the UMAC library (STA)

Stock: a TWT Setup frame from the AP is received and dropped.

  • Frame routing. TWT Setup frames are now passed to the TWT module instead of the relay handler that dropped them, on an open BSS as well as on a protected one.
  • Sender check. The transmitter must be the AP that the STA is associated with. A frame from any other sender is dropped, so that no other device in range can change the STA's schedule.
  • Update of a live agreement. An Accept received mid-session replaces the agreement and re-installs it in the radio firmware, without disconnecting. This holds for any setup command the STA used at association, whereas the stock code adopted the AP's parameters only after a Suggest.
  • All three parameters. The stock code adopts only the target wake time. When built with TWT_ADOPT=1, the STA also adopts the wake interval and the minimum wake duration.

in umac_datapath.c, s1g_action.c and umac_twt.c

1 s

Two field encodings limit the values that can be commanded. The wake interval is encoded as mantissa × 2exponent, with a 16-bit mantissa and a 5-bit exponent: 62500 × 24 gives exactly 1,000,000 µs. The minimum wake duration is one octet in units of 256 µs, hence the longest SP that can be signalled is 255 × 256 = 65,280 µs, which is the value used in this testbed. The target wake time is a 64-bit value in microseconds and is not quantized.

The setup command Accept is normally the response of the AP to a TWT request from a STA. Here, the AP sends an unsolicited Accept that carries its own parameters, and the STA adopts them. This is how the AP dictates a schedule without any change to the frame format.

5Signalling path from the AP to the STA

Fig. 6 follows one schedule update from the host to the radio firmware of the STA. The hostapd daemon on the AP and the TCP/IP stack on the STA are not involved. The driver hands the frame to mac80211 instead of sending it straight to the chip, so that it goes through the same queuing as data frames and is transmitted while the STA is awake. When the frame was sent directly to the chip, which is the original path in the driver, only 2 of 20 updates were delivered, since the STA is asleep most of the time.

The AP commits an update only after it is acknowledged. More specifically, the driver replaces its own record of a STA's schedule only when the chip reports that the STA acknowledged the frame, so that a lost frame does not leave the AP holding a schedule that the STA never received. In addition, each update is transmitted three times, one wake interval apart, and the driver counts every transmission as confirmed, unacknowledged or expired. These counters are shown on the dashboard (Section 10).

7From the TWT agreement to the sleep/wake schedule

Once the agreement is installed, the timing is executed by the radio firmware. The firmware keeps its TSF timer synchronized with the AP through the AP's beacons (beacon interval of 102.4 ms). It wakes the radio when the TSF reaches the target wake time, keeps it awake for at least the minimum wake duration, and then returns it to sleep. Every next SP starts one wake interval later, without any further signalling; this is the implicit TWT operation.

Therefore, a target wake time of 166,000 µs with a wake interval of 1 s places the SP 166 ms into every second of the AP's TSF. While the STA is asleep, its uplink packets wait in a queue. When it wakes up, it transmits them in a burst that occupies most of the SP, as shown in Fig. 7.

Fig. 7. One SP of sta2, as captured by the sniffer on 18 November 2026. Each tick is one PPDU on air. The SP starts a few milliseconds after the nominal wake time (2.55 ms in this capture, the same for every STA), and the burst ends a few milliseconds before the end of the SP.

Fig. 8.

A new schedule takes effect a few seconds after the update is sent: the STA keeps its old schedule and then switches to the new one in a single step. In the runs of 18 November 2026, every schedule change settled within 3 to 6 s, without re-association: by then the AP's agreement table held the commanded wake time, interval and duration for every STA, all acknowledged. However, a schedule does not survive a re-association: a STA that associates again is assigned a slot by the AP driver's own allocator, so the testbed pushes the schedule again after any restart.

8Over-the-air verification

The AP's agreement table only shows what the AP has stored, not what the STAs do. On this testbed, the AP has reported a clean schedule while the STAs were in fact waking up at the same time. Therefore, every schedule is verified from the air by a second, independent radio, in four steps.

1 · Capture
The sniffer stays on the same channel in monitor mode and records every frame it receives, with a timestamp from its own clock.
2 · Timebase
Every beacon carries the AP's TSF, so a linear fit over the beacons of a capture (about 135 in 25 s) converts the sniffer's timestamps to AP time, with a residual of a few microseconds.
3 · Folding
The timestamp of every data frame is converted to AP time and folded modulo the 1 s wake interval, per STA.
4 · Scoring
(i) Does the complete burst of each STA, including the airtime of its last PPDU, fit inside its SP? (ii) What fraction of its data frames falls inside the SP?

Table 3. Off-air results of 18 November 2026, 25 s per capture.

STAs that share an SP contend with each other, so in cfg2 and cfg3 one STA obtained much less airtime than the others and produced too few frames to be scored. The 2 to 3% of the data outside the SP are frames just ahead of its estimated start, which is anchored at the 2nd percentile of each STA's frames; no data frame was observed past the end of an SP.

10Live dashboard

The dashboard runs on the host and shows two views of the network side by side: the AP's own state, in indigo, and what the sniffer captured on air, in teal. A schedule is taken to be in effect only where the two agree. Each STA keeps the same colour in every panel.

The dashboard listens only on the host itself, since its buttons reconfigure the radios without any login. To view it from another machine, forward the port over SSH and open http://localhost:8787:

Future work

The testbed currently switches among preset configurations. The next step is to put a learned scheduler in control of it: a policy trained in simulation selects the TWT schedule, and the same one-line-per-STA interface delivers it to the STAs. The energy saving of a schedule has not been measured on the boards yet; this requires a current measurement at a STA and is planned next.
